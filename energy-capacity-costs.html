Utilities pay for energy production and peak-day grid capacity.

A customer bill is easy to read as a charge for electricity used, but utility economics split into two different cost problems: producing or buying each unit of electricity, and expanding or reserving enough grid capacity for the hottest, highest-demand summer days.

Cost primer

Core distinction Production cost is incremental; capacity cost is lumpy. Every unit served has a supply cost. A few extreme summer hours can require grid upgrades, capacity contracts, and reserves that are paid for all year.
Production cost driver Each unit served
Capacity cost driver Peak summer days
Rate-design lever Avoid costly peaks
1

Production costs accumulate

Every unit of electricity has to be generated or purchased. That cost varies by hour as fuel, wholesale prices, and system stress change.

2

Capacity costs come from rare peaks

Hot summer afternoons can determine how much feeder, transformer, generation, and reserve capacity the utility must stand ready to provide.

3

Rates can avoid expensive capacity

Time-varying prices and demand charges are useful when they reduce expensive supply hours or defer the next capacity upgrade.

Capacity constraint

Peak shifting can avoid a grid build.

A tariff can look attractive on revenue but still be costly if it pushes a summer peak above existing grid capability. Moving some load into nearby shoulder hours can keep the hottest peak below the ceiling and avoid capacity investment.

Production cost

Paying to produce or buy each unit of electricity.

Production cost is the variable cost of serving electricity. It rises with each unit delivered and can be much higher during stressed hours when the utility relies on expensive generation or wholesale purchases.

Cost pattern Incremental
Driven by How much is served
High when Supply prices spike 
production cost = electricity served x hourly supply cost
Peak capacity cost

Paying to expand or reserve the grid for rare summer peaks.

Capacity cost is a readiness cost. A small number of hot summer hours can require more wires, transformers, generation contracts, or reserves even if ordinary-day energy use is not very different.

Cost pattern Lumpy
Driven by Peak summer demand
High when Grid ceiling is hit 
capacity cost = peak-day shortfall x upgrade or reserve cost

The practical distinction

Two rate designs can require similar energy production, but create different utility economics if one pushes the system toward a summer capacity expansion.

Each unit served Generation or market purchases create variable cost.
Peak summer day The system must be expanded or reserved for rare extremes.
Managerial value Peak reduction can defer a grid build or capacity contract.

How this connects to the rate options

1
Flat rate

Simple and stable, but weak at distinguishing ordinary consumption from hours that push the grid toward expansion.

2
Time-of-use rate

Makes high-cost supply hours visible, especially when hot summer peaks drive expensive generation or market purchases.

3
Demand charge

Charges for contribution to peak-day capacity need, which can better align bills with grid expansion pressure.

How to read it in this model

The dashboard uses simplified cost proxies, but the distinction is the same one utilities use when they ask whether a rate design only changes customer bills or actually avoids production and peak-capacity costs.
