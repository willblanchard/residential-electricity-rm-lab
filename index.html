Electricity pricing is a revenue-management problem.

The project asks how a residential utility should design rate plans when customers differ in willingness to pay, technology adoption, and ability to shift load. A rate can reduce customer bills and still improve utility economics if it avoids enough high-cost evening energy and scarce capacity.

Project question

Managerial decision Which rate menu should the utility offer? Compare revenue, cost, margin, demand response, and leakage before making a pricing recommendation.
Single price One plan
Parallel options Many plans
Core risk Leakage

Regulatory context

The utility is modeled as a regulated residential provider, not a competitive retailer. Rates must be simple enough to defend in a public utility commission filing and stable enough to recover costs over a multi-year rate case.

Regulated monopoly One approved tariff applies to a customer class rather than being negotiated customer by customer.
Four objectives Recover allowed costs, keep peak demand within grid limits, avoid unfair cost shifts, and stay implementable.
Three rate designs Flat, energy plus capacity, and TOU pricing are compared as increasingly complex ways to send scarcity signals.

Project logic

Start with customer heterogeneity, convert it into a rate-design model, test one-plan pricing, then test whether multiple plans still work once customers can self-select.

Inputs Customer segments, WTP curve, load profiles, device response, and tariff candidates.
Optimization Choose a rate structure that balances revenue neutrality, cost avoidance, and capacity pressure.
Decision Recommend the rate-menu design that improves margin without creating too much leakage.
